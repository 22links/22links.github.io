1984
An original 1949 copy of the book "Nineteen Eighty-Four" by George Orwell.
George Orwell’s “Nineteen Eighty-Four” (published in 1949) was a speculative fiction novel that presents a dystopian hypothesis of the state of the world in it’s namesake year, 1984. The novel explores the themes of totalitarianism, mass surveillance, and repression. In it, society exists under a regime where power is centralised within the state, stripping individuals of their autonomy and agency. It's ruling figure, the ominous “Big Brother,” exercises this control through ownership over people’s perception of reality. Read:
"THE PARTY TOLD YOU TO REJECT THE EVIDENCE OF YOUR EYES AND EARS. IT WAS THEIR FINAL, MOST ESSENTIAL COMMAND."
Notably, this rule was enacted through technology (two-way televisions, hidden cameras, and microphones) functioning as tools of constant surveillance and control. Through these systems, individuals were monitored, influenced, and regulated. Orwell’s vision suggested a world in which media actively constructed reality, rather than exist as a reflection of it.

NAM JUNE PAIK
An excerpt from Good Morning, Mr. Orwell
“Good Morning, Mr. Orwell” was the brainchild of South Korean-born American artist Nam June Paik, presented in the form of an international satellite “installation”. The event connected New York television studio WNET TV with it’s Paris-based peer Centre Pompidou via satellite, facilitating a combined broadcast from the two stations through real-time communication.
Good Morning, Mr. Orwell
The broadcast took place specifically on New Years Day, 1984 to serve as a direct response to Orwell's dystopian vision. Paik held a more optimistic view. Through “Good Morning, Mr Orwell”, he wrestles the powerful, exclusive tool of satellite transmission away from systems of control, coercion and surveillance to rethink the potential this technology holds for mass communication. He contends for it’s use as an “art-commons” where people can mutually communicate through arts and culture.
